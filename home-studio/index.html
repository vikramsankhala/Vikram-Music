Before You Hit Record

Setup Tips

1

Treat the Room First

Install YGM foam panels on walls facing your monitors before recording anything. A treated room will do more for your recordings than any plugin chain. Place isolation pads under HS5s to decouple them from the desk.

2

Gain Staging Matters

Set input gain on the Scarlett Solo so your signal peaks around -18 dBFS during normal performance. This gives you headroom and keeps digital clipping out of the picture. The MG10XU's channel faders should sit near unity.

3

Use the Right Mic for the Job

The SM58 is your workhorse for loud sources and live takes — its cardioid pattern naturally rejects room noise. The AT2020 condenser shines for acoustic guitars, voiceovers, and any source needing more air and detail.

4

Monitor at Low Volumes

Reference your mixes at the quietest level where you can still clearly hear every element. The HS5s are revealing monitors — ear fatigue is your enemy. Use the M50x for long mixing sessions to protect your hearing.

5

5

Pop Filter Placement

Position the Kadence pop filter about 5–7 cm in front of your microphone. Sing or speak across it slightly — not directly into it. This kills plosives without dulling your vocal tone.

6

MIDI Keyboard Workflow

Connect the MPK Mini via USB directly to your computer — no interface needed. Map the 8 pads to drum sounds or plugin triggers. Use the 25 keys for melodic parts and chord sketching. Assign knobs to plugin parameters in your DAW.

Know Your Tools

Gear Explained

01

Focusrite Scarlett Solo 4th Gen

Audio Interface

₹17,367  ·  The Brain of Your Studio

The Scarlett Solo is the digital gateway between your analogue world and your computer. It converts microphone and instrument signals into digital audio your DAW can record and process. The 4th Generation model brings Focusrite's best-ever preamp design — the Air mode adds a subtle high-frequency lift inspired by their classic ISA transformer preamps, giving vocals more presence and sheen without EQ. It connects via USB-C and is class-compliant, meaning no driver installation is needed on most systems.

1× XLR/TRS Combo Input 1× Instrument Input 48V Phantom Power Air Mode USB-C 24-bit / 192kHz

Role in the studio: Every microphone you record (SM58, AT2020) passes through the Scarlett Solo on its way into your DAW. The Yamaha MG10XU's main mix output also feeds into the Solo when you want to record a blended mix or use the mixer as a preamp stage. The Solo's monitor outputs feed the HS5 monitors via TRS cables.

02

Yamaha MG10XU 10-Channel Mixer

Mixer

₹17,499  ·  Routing Hub & Live Control

The MG10XU is an analogue mixing console with 4 mono XLR/TRS combo inputs and 3 stereo line-level channels. Each mono channel has its own 3-band EQ (high, mid, low), a high-pass filter to cut rumble below 80Hz, a gain knob for setting input level, and a send to the built-in studio-grade effects processor. The onboard SPX effects unit provides reverb, chorus, and delay. Crucially, it also has a built-in USB audio interface — but for this studio, the Focusrite Solo handles the primary computer connection at higher quality.

4× Mono XLR/TRS 3× Stereo Line 3-Band EQ per Channel Built-in SPX Effects 2× Aux Sends USB Audio

Role in the studio: Use the MG10XU to manage multiple sources simultaneously — plug in both microphones, a keyboard, and a backing track playback device. Its main stereo output feeds the Focusrite Solo. When recording vocals with a backing track, the mixer lets you blend the two for a headphone cue mix without the performer hearing only a dry signal.

03

Shure SM58-LC Cardioid Vocal Mic

Dynamic Microphone

₹10,499  ·  The World's Most Trusted Vocal Mic

The SM58 is a dynamic microphone — it uses a moving coil inside a magnetic field to convert sound waves to electrical signal, requiring no external power. Its cardioid polar pattern picks up sound primarily from the front and naturally rejects sound from the rear and sides, making it ideal for home studios where room reflections and background noise are concerns. It has a built-in spherical wire mesh grille with an integrated pop filter, and a contoured frequency response with a presence peak around 5kHz that adds clarity to vocals.

Dynamic Cardioid Pattern No Phantom Power Required 50Hz–15kHz XLR Output

Role in the studio: Primary vocal microphone for singing, rap, podcasting, and any loud or dynamic source. Its ruggedness means you can use it close to loud sources (guitar amps, loud singers) without damage. It connects via XLR cable to any channel on the MG10XU. No phantom power needed — just plug in and sing.

04

Audio-Technica AT2020 Condenser Mic

Condenser Microphone

₹8,499  ·  Detail, Air & Studio Clarity

The AT2020 is a large-diaphragm condenser microphone. Unlike dynamic mics, condensers use a thin electrically charged diaphragm suspended near a backplate — this design is far more sensitive and captures a much wider frequency range, including the delicate high-frequency air and transient detail that dynamic mics miss. Because of this, it requires 48V phantom power from your interface or mixer. Its extended frequency response (20Hz–20kHz) and low self-noise make it ideal for capturing acoustic instruments, room ambience, voiceovers, and soft-spoken dialogue.

Large Diaphragm Condenser Cardioid Pattern Requires 48V Phantom Power 20Hz–20kHz Low Self-Noise: 20dB SPL

Role in the studio: Your detail and nuance microphone. Mount it on the Kadence boom arm, position it for acoustic guitar, vocals in a treated corner, or any source where you want maximum fidelity. Always enable phantom power (+48V) on the MG10XU channel before plugging in, and wait 30 seconds before switching on — condensers are more sensitive to power surges.

05

Yamaha HS5 Studio Monitors (Pair)

Studio Monitors

₹32,990  ·  Reference-Grade Accuracy

The HS5 is an active (powered) near-field studio monitor. Unlike consumer speakers that colour sound to make music sound pleasing, studio monitors are designed to be flat and accurate — they reveal problems in your mix rather than hiding them. Each HS5 has a 5-inch woofer and a 1-inch tweeter, both with their own built-in amplifiers. The HS series is famous for its "white woofer" design inherited from Yamaha's legendary NS-10 studio monitors used in recording studios worldwide for decades. The HS5 includes room control and high-trim response switches on the back to adjust for placement near walls.

5" Woofer + 1" Tweeter Active / Bi-Amplified TRS / XLR Input 54Hz–30kHz 45W + 25W Amplifiers Room Control EQ

Role in the studio: Place them at ear height on the MOPAD isolation pads, positioned in an equilateral triangle with your head (each monitor and your listening position forming the three corners, each side about 1–1.2m). They receive signal from the Scarlett Solo's monitor outputs via balanced TRS cables. Use the back-panel room EQ switches if placed near walls: −2dB or −4dB on the Low cut, and adjust High Trim for brightness.

06

Audio-Technica ATH-M50x Headphones

Studio Headphones

₹10,799  ·  Critical Listening & Mix Reference

The ATH-M50x are closed-back, circumaural (around-ear) studio headphones with 45mm large-aperture drivers. Closed-back design means the ear cups are sealed, preventing sound from leaking out — critical when recording with a microphone, since open headphones would bleed sound into your recording. The M50x is one of the most widely used reference headphones in the world, renowned for its accurate, detailed sound across the full frequency range. They come with three detachable cables — coiled, straight long, and straight short — and fold flat for storage.

Closed-Back 45mm Drivers 15Hz–28kHz 38Ω Impedance Detachable Cable Foldable

Role in the studio: Primary headphones for critical listening, mix referencing, and long mixing sessions. Connect to the Scarlett Solo's front headphone output (which has its own dedicated amplifier). When a vocalist is recording, plug the M50x into the mixer's phones output so they hear a cue blend of their voice and the backing track without monitor bleed.

07

Audio-Technica ATH-M20x Headphones

Studio Headphones

₹4,399  ·  Performer Cue Headphones

The ATH-M20x are the entry-level offering in Audio-Technica's professional monitor series, sharing the same closed-back circumaural design as the M50x but with 40mm drivers. They are excellent value for money and provide accurate monitoring for tracking sessions. Their closed-back design prevents headphone bleed into the microphone. These are your "second pair" — kept on the desk as a quick alternative reference or handed to a collaborating performer during tracking.

Closed-Back 40mm Drivers 20Hz–20kHz 47Ω Impedance Fixed Cable

Role in the studio: Performer cue headphones during recording sessions. While the M50x stays on your head at the engineering position, the M20x goes to the vocalist or instrumentalist. Plug it into the MG10XU's second headphone output (Phones B) for an independent cue mix. Also useful as a quick reference check — differences between the M50x and M20x reveal low-mid frequency translation issues in mixes.

08

Akai MPK Mini MK4

MIDI Controller

₹8,550  ·  Compose, Trigger & Control

The MPK Mini MK4 is a class-compliant USB MIDI controller — it generates MIDI data (Musical Instrument Digital Interface), not audio. MIDI is a communication protocol that tells software instruments what notes to play, how hard, and for how long. The MK4 features 25 mini velocity-sensitive keys, 8 RGB MPC-style velocity-sensitive pads, 8 assignable knobs, and a built-in arpeggiator and chord mode. A new addition on the MK4 is the 5-way joystick for pitch bend and modulation, replacing the previous thumbstick design.

25 Mini Keys 8 RGB Pads 8 Assignable Knobs Arpeggiator Chord Mode USB-A (no driver needed)

Role in the studio: Connect directly to your computer via USB. In your DAW, create a MIDI track and set the MPK Mini as the input device. Load a virtual instrument (soft synth, piano, drums) on the track and play — the keys control pitched instruments while the 8 pads are ideal for triggering drum hits. The 8 knobs can be mapped to plugin parameters like filter cutoff, reverb send, or volume faders for hands-on control.

09

Kadence NB35-POP Boom Arm + Pop Filter

Rigging

₹699  ·  Positioning & Plosive Control

The NB35 is a scissor-arm studio boom arm that clamps to any desk edge and positions your microphone exactly where you need it — no stand needed on the floor. It features internal spring tension for smooth, stable positioning and cable management channels to keep XLR cables tidy. The included pop filter is a circular frame with a fine mesh screen that mounts on a flexible gooseneck. Pop filters diffuse the sudden bursts of air that cause low-frequency plosive thumps (the "P" and "B" sounds that overload microphone capsules) before they reach the mic.

Scissor Arm Design Desk Clamp Mount 5/8" Mic Thread 360° Swivel Head Pop Filter Included

Role in the studio: Clamp to the left or right desk edge. Thread the XLR cable through the arm's internal channel for a clean setup. Mount either the SM58 or AT2020, then position the pop filter 5–7cm in front of the mic capsule. The boom arm lets you record sitting naturally rather than hunching toward a floor stand, reducing physical fatigue during long sessions.

10

Hawk Proaudio XLR & TRS Cables

Signal Cables

₹699 XLR  ·  ₹521–₹899 TRS (×2)

XLR cables carry balanced audio using three conductors — positive signal, negative signal, and ground. This balanced configuration means any noise picked up along the cable length is automatically cancelled out at the destination (common-mode rejection), making XLR ideal for long microphone runs and professional interconnects. TRS (Tip-Ring-Sleeve) cables in balanced configuration serve the same purpose for line-level signals — they connect the Focusrite Solo's monitor outputs to each Yamaha HS5. Balanced connections should always be preferred over unbalanced in studio wiring.

XLR: 3-Pin Balanced TRS: 6.35mm Balanced Noise-Rejecting Mic & Line Level

Role in the studio: XLR cable runs from microphone to mixer. Two TRS cables run from the Scarlett Solo's left and right monitor outputs to the left and right HS5 monitors respectively. Always use balanced cables wherever the equipment supports it — you will immediately notice less hum and interference compared to unbalanced connections.

11

YGM Acoustic Foam + MOPAD Isolation Pads

Acoustic Treatment

₹1,295 Foam + ₹1,276 MOPADs + ₹773 Adhesive

Acoustic foam panels absorb mid and high-frequency sound waves, reducing the time it takes for sound to decay in a room (reverberation time). Without treatment, every recording you make captures not just your voice or instrument but also the reflections bouncing off walls, floor, and ceiling — creating a boxy, echoey sound that no plugin can fully remove. The YGM panels work by converting sound energy into heat through friction as the waves travel through the open-cell foam structure. The MOPAD isolation pads (Monitor Isolation Pads) are high-density polyurethane wedges that decouple the HS5 monitors from the desk surface, preventing vibration from transferring and creating bass resonance.

18 Foam Panels Mid/High Absorption MOPAD Decoupling Pads Spray Adhesive Mount

Role in the studio: Install foam panels on the wall directly behind your monitors (front wall), on the two side walls at the "first reflection points" (where sound from the monitors bounces to your ears), and on the ceiling above your mix position. Place MOPADs under each HS5. This is the single highest-impact upgrade you can make to recording quality — do this before your first take.

Day One, Done Right

Complete Setup Procedure

1

Room Preparation & Acoustic Treatment

Before any gear is powered on — lay the acoustic foundation

▼
1

Choose & Clear Your Recording Corner

Select a room corner for your primary recording position — corners are acoustically "dead" and naturally reduce reflections. Avoid rooms with hard parallel walls and no furnishings. A bedroom with a wardrobe, bookshelf, and mattress already has natural diffusion. Clear at least 1.5m in front of where your desk will sit.

2

Identify First Reflection Points

Sit in your intended listening/mix position. Have someone hold a small mirror flat against the left side wall and slide it forward from behind you until you can see the reflection of your left monitor in it — that is your left first reflection point. Repeat for the right wall and ceiling above you. Mark these three spots with painter's tape.

Pro tip: These three points are the most critical positions for acoustic foam. Reflections arriving here interfere with the direct sound from your monitors and cause frequency smearing that makes mixes translate poorly to other systems.
3

Apply Foam Panels with Fevicol Ezee Spray

Shake the Fevicol Ezee spray can well. Working in a ventilated area, lightly spray the back of each YGM foam panel and the wall area where it will go. Wait 45–60 seconds for the adhesive to become tacky, then press firmly for 10–15 seconds. Start with the three first reflection points (2 side walls, 1 ceiling), then place remaining panels symmetrically on the front wall behind your future monitor position. Space panels evenly rather than clustering — distributed coverage is more effective than a solid block.

Important: Do not spray adhesive directly onto painted drywall without testing a small hidden patch first. The spray is repositionable for about 30 seconds but bonds permanently after that. Allow 2 hours to fully cure before placing anything against the panels.
4

Position the Desk

Place your desk so your listening position sits roughly 38% of the room's length from the front wall — this is the standard acoustic sweet spot that avoids the worst bass buildup modes in rectangular rooms. Your back should face the corner, not a flat wall. Leave at least 50cm between your desk and the front wall where the monitors will sit.

The acoustic treatment phase directly impacts every other piece of gear. Untreated rooms add 6–12dB of frequency irregularity at your listening position, meaning what you hear in the monitors is not what's actually in the recording. No amount of EQ, compression, or monitoring quality overcomes a bad room — acoustic treatment is always first.

2

Monitor & Headphone Setup

Position, connect and calibrate your monitoring chain

▼
1

Place MOPAD Isolation Pads

Set one MOPAD on each side of your desk where the HS5 monitors will sit. The foam wedge side faces downward. The angled top surface slightly tilts the monitors upward — position them so the tweeter (the small top driver) will be at ear height when you're seated. This ensures the high frequencies arrive at your ears directly without desk reflection.

2

Position HS5 Monitors in an Equilateral Triangle

Place each HS5 on its MOPAD. The left monitor goes to your left, right to your right. The distance between the two monitors should equal the distance from each monitor to your head — forming a perfect equilateral triangle. For a typical desk this is 80–120cm per side. Angle each monitor inward so it points directly at your listening position (toe them in). The tweeters should be exactly at ear level.

Pro tip: Measure the triangle with a tape measure rather than eyeballing it. Even 5cm asymmetry can throw off stereo imaging and make mixes sound wider on one side.
3

Set Room Control Switches on HS5 Rear Panel

Each HS5 has two switches on the back. Room Control cuts low frequencies to compensate for bass buildup near walls: set to 0dB if your desk is more than 1m from any wall, −2dB if within 1m, −4dB if the monitor is close to a corner. High Trim adjusts treble response: 0dB for standard rooms, +0.5dB or +1dB if the room sounds dull and absorptive, −0.5dB or −1dB if it sounds harsh and bright. Set both monitors identically.

4

Connect Monitors to Focusrite Scarlett Solo

Use a TRS cable for each monitor. Connect one end of the first TRS cable to the Monitor L output on the Scarlett Solo's rear panel, and the other end to the left HS5's TRS input. Repeat with the second TRS cable for the Monitor R output to the right HS5. Keep the monitor volume knob on the Scarlett Solo at minimum for now — do not power on the monitors yet.

Important: Always connect all cables before powering on monitors. Plugging cables into a live system can cause damaging signal pops (transients) through the drivers.
5

Connect Headphones

Plug the ATH-M50x into the Scarlett Solo's front-panel headphone jack (6.35mm). Plug the ATH-M20x into the Yamaha MG10XU's headphone output. This configuration means you can monitor through the interface while a performer monitors through the mixer — giving each person an independent mix without sharing a single headphone output.

The monitoring chain flows: DAW audio output → Scarlett Solo → HS5 monitors (for speaker monitoring) and Scarlett Solo → ATH-M50x (for headphone monitoring). The Scarlett Solo's monitor volume knob controls both simultaneously. The MG10XU headphone output is a separate, parallel output for performer cue mixes — it doesn't go through the Focusrite.

3

Mixer Setup & Routing

Configure the Yamaha MG10XU for your studio workflow

▼
1

Set Initial Mixer State to Zero / Unity

Before powering on, set every channel fader to the unity (0dB) position — marked with a line on the fader scale. Set the master fader to unity as well. Turn all gain (trim) knobs fully counter-clockwise (minimum). Set all EQ knobs to their centre (12 o'clock) position, which is flat/neutral. Turn the aux send knobs to minimum. This "zeroed" starting state prevents unexpected loud signals when you power up.

2

Power on the MG10XU

Plug the power adapter into the rear IEC socket and switch the power on at the rear panel switch. The PFL/SOLO and SIG/PEAK LEDs on each channel will briefly flash — this is normal self-test behaviour. The mixer is now on. Do not connect microphones yet if phantom power is on for any channel.

3

Enable Phantom Power for AT2020

Decide which channel the AT2020 will occupy — Channel 1 or 2 is recommended. Press the +48V (phantom power) button on that channel — a small LED will illuminate. Now connect the AT2020's XLR cable to that channel. Wait 30 seconds before raising the channel gain. This delay allows the condenser's capsule to fully charge before signal flows.

Important: Never plug or unplug the AT2020 while phantom power is on. The SM58 does not require phantom power and will not be harmed by it, but condenser capsules can be damaged by hot-plugging (inserting while phantom is active). Always: switch phantom on → wait 30 seconds → plug in mic → raise gain. To unplug: reduce gain → switch phantom off → wait 30 seconds → unplug.
4

Connect SM58 to Second Mic Channel

Plug the XLR cable into the SM58's XLR output (bottom of the mic body). Connect the other end to Channel 2 on the MG10XU. The SM58 requires no phantom power — it will work on any channel. Route the XLR cable through the Kadence boom arm's internal cable management channel if both mics share the arm.

5

Connect Mixer Main Output to Scarlett Solo

Run a TRS cable from the MG10XU's Main Mix L/R output into the Scarlett Solo's line input. This creates the pathway: microphone → mixer preamp/EQ → Focusrite conversion → your DAW. Alternatively, for simpler single-mic sessions, plug a microphone directly into the Scarlett Solo's combo input and bypass the mixer entirely — both workflows are valid.

6

Set Gain Structure on Each Channel

With a mic connected and the performer speaking or singing at performance volume, slowly raise the channel's Gain knob. Watch the SIG/PEAK LED: you want the green signal LED flashing consistently (signal present) but the red peak LED never lighting. A red peak LED means the preamp is clipping — reduce gain. Aim for the LED to be solidly green with only occasional amber on the loudest hits.

Pro tip: The MG10XU has a PFL (pre-fader listen) button per channel. Press it to solo that channel through the headphone output at its gain-stage level, letting you set gain precisely without affecting the main mix output.

The mixer serves as both a multi-source combiner and a headphone cue manager. Its main output feeds the interface. Its headphone output feeds the performer. These are two completely independent outputs, so you can send the performer a different mix than what's going to the DAW — for example, adding reverb to the vocalist's headphone mix (so they perform better) while recording a completely dry signal into the DAW for maximum flexibility in post-production.

4

Interface & Computer Setup

Install drivers, configure the DAW, set sample rate and buffer

▼
1

Connect Scarlett Solo to Computer

Use the included USB-C cable to connect the Scarlett Solo to your computer. On modern macOS (10.14+) and Windows 10/11, the Scarlett Solo is class-compliant and will be automatically detected without drivers. The Solo's LED halo will glow green when connected. For the best experience, download Focusrite Control from the Focusrite website — this free app unlocks the Air mode, direct monitoring mix settings, and the main mix output level.

2

Set System Audio Output to Scarlett Solo

On macOS: System Settings → Sound → Output → select Scarlett Solo USB. On Windows: right-click the speaker icon → Sound Settings → Output device → Scarlett Solo USB. This routes all system audio through the interface. Now all computer audio — YouTube, Spotify, notifications — will play through your HS5 monitors. Set the system volume to 75–80% and use the Scarlett Solo's physical monitor knob for day-to-day volume control.

3

Install a DAW (Digital Audio Workstation)

A DAW is the software studio where you record, edit, arrange, and mix audio. Recommended free options: GarageBand (macOS only — excellent for beginners, included free), Cakewalk by BandLab (Windows only, fully professional, free), or Reaper (Windows/Mac, ₹4,600 licence after free trial, industry-standard). For serious music production, Ableton Live Intro (often bundled with the Scarlett Solo as a free download via Focusrite's software bundle) is excellent. Install your chosen DAW before proceeding.

4

Configure Audio Settings Inside the DAW

In your DAW's preferences or audio settings, set: Audio Device → Scarlett Solo USB. Sample Rate → 44100 Hz (for music) or 48000 Hz (for video/podcast). Buffer Size → start at 128 samples for recording (low latency), increase to 256 or 512 samples during mixing (lower CPU load). On macOS, select Core Audio as the driver. On Windows, select ASIO and install Focusrite's ASIO driver for the lowest possible latency.

Pro tip: Latency is the delay between you singing into the mic and hearing yourself in the headphones. Buffer size controls latency: 128 samples at 44.1kHz = ~6ms delay, which feels imperceptible. 512 samples = ~23ms, which feels like a slight echo and makes performing uncomfortable. Always record at 128 samples buffer.
5

Test the Signal Chain End-to-End

Create a new audio track in your DAW. Set its input to Input 1 (Mono) — the Scarlett Solo's main XLR input. Arm the track for recording (click the red record arm button on the track). Speak into the microphone. You should see the input level meter in the DAW moving. If it's not moving, check: Is the mic plugged in? Is the gain knob raised? Is the correct input selected in the DAW? Is the interface selected as the audio device? Solve each potential failure point one at a time.

6

Enable Direct Monitoring (Zero-Latency Playback)

The Scarlett Solo has a Direct Monitor button (headphone icon with a split circle). When enabled, it routes the incoming microphone signal directly to the headphone output at hardware level — bypassing the computer entirely. This gives true zero-latency monitoring. Enable it when recording. Disable it when you want to hear your recorded tracks playing back through the DAW (e.g., during mixing).

The Scarlett Solo is the digital hub — everything converts here. Analogue audio in, digital audio to the DAW. Digital playback from the DAW, analogue audio out to monitors and headphones. The sample rate must match across the interface settings, the DAW settings, and any video you're scoring — mismatches cause pitch shifting and timing errors.

Focusrite's free software bundle (accessible via your Focusrite account after registering the Solo) includes Ableton Live Lite, Pro Tools Artist (3-month trial), and a selection of plugin instruments and effects — activate all of these. They significantly expand your studio's capabilities at no extra cost.

5

Microphone Positioning & Boom Arm

Mount, angle and position for optimal capture

▼
1

Mount the Kadence Boom Arm

Open the clamp at the base of the NB35-POP and slide it over the left or right edge of your desk. Tighten the thumbscrew until firmly clamped — it should not rotate or shift when you extend the arm. Thread the XLR cable through the arm before attaching the microphone, using the internal channel or the external cable clips.

2

Attach the Microphone

The boom arm's mic end has a standard 5/8" thread. The SM58 clips into its clip adapter (included with the mic) which then screws onto the thread. The AT2020 screws directly onto a 5/8" adapter or uses its included thread adapter. Tighten until snug but don't overtighten — you need to be able to adjust the angle. Connect the XLR cable to the mic's output.

3

Position the Pop Filter

Attach the Kadence pop filter's gooseneck clamp to the boom arm, near the mic end. Bend the gooseneck so the filter's mesh screen sits 5–7cm directly in front of the mic capsule. The screen should be perpendicular to the direction the mic points. Do not press the filter against the mic — the gap is essential for it to function correctly.

4

For Vocals: Set Mic Position

Position the mic at mouth height, angled very slightly downward (15°) so you sing slightly across it rather than directly into it. This further reduces plosives and sibilance. Aim for 15–25cm working distance for a detailed, intimate vocal sound. Closer produces more proximity effect (bass boost) — use this creatively for warmth. Farther captures more room but sacrifices presence.

Pro tip: Sing slightly off-axis (5–10° to one side of centre) to reduce proximity effect and sibilance while retaining presence. Small changes in mic angle make enormous differences in sound — experiment while watching your recording level in the DAW.
5

For Acoustic Guitar: Set Mic Position

Remove the pop filter — it's not needed for instruments. Point the AT2020 at the guitar's 12th fret (where the neck meets the body), approximately 20–30cm away, angled slightly toward the soundhole. This position captures a balanced blend of string attack (from the fret area) and body resonance (from the soundhole) without the excessive bass boom of pointing directly into the soundhole.

6

MIDI Keyboard & Production Setup

Connect, configure and map the Akai MPK Mini MK4

▼
1

Connect the MPK Mini via USB

Connect the Akai MPK Mini MK4 to a USB-A port on your computer using the included cable. It requires no driver — it is class-compliant and will appear in your DAW's MIDI device list immediately. The keyboard does not need to go through the audio interface — it communicates MIDI data over USB, not audio.

2

Configure in the DAW

In your DAW's preferences, go to MIDI settings and enable the MPK Mini MK4 as a MIDI input device. Create a new instrument track (not audio track). Set the track's MIDI input to MPK Mini MK4 — All Channels. Load a virtual instrument plugin (a built-in piano, synth, or drum kit) on the track. Arm the track for recording. Play the keys — you should now hear sound through your monitors.

3

Configure the 8 Pads for Drums

The 8 pads send MIDI note messages. By default they're set to trigger notes C1–G1, which in most drum plugins correspond to kick, snare, hi-hats, and cymbals. Load a drum instrument (GarageBand's Drummer, Addictive Drums, or any drum plugin). The pads will immediately trigger drum hits. You can customise which drum sound each pad triggers by changing the pad's MIDI note assignment in the MPK Mini's editor software (downloadable free from Akai's website).

4

Map the 8 Knobs to Plugin Parameters

The 8 assignable knobs send MIDI CC (Continuous Controller) messages. In most DAWs you can right-click any plugin knob (reverb size, filter cutoff, etc.) and select "MIDI Learn" or "Assign Controller", then turn the physical MPK knob you want to control it. This gives you hands-on physical control over plugin parameters without touching the mouse — significantly improving the feel of your workflow.

Pro tip: Map Knob 1 to your primary reverb's mix/send level, Knob 2 to the delay mix, and Knobs 3–4 to your virtual synth's filter cutoff and resonance. These are the parameters you'll reach for most often during performance and live arrangement.
5

Use Chord Mode and Arpeggiator

The MPK Mini MK4 has a built-in Chord mode (press the Chord button) — pressing a single key triggers a full chord. The factory chord presets include major and minor triads, 7th chords, and sus2 chords. This is invaluable if your keyboard playing is limited. The Arpeggiator (press Arp button) takes the notes you hold and plays them in a rhythmic pattern — set the rate (1/4, 1/8, 1/16 notes) using the BPM/Time Division controls. The arp syncs to your DAW's tempo if MIDI clock is enabled.

The MIDI keyboard operates completely independently from the audio recording chain. Audio from your DAW's virtual instruments exits through the Scarlett Solo (since the DAW's output is set to the Scarlett) and plays through the HS5 monitors — so MIDI performance and audio monitoring are seamlessly integrated even though the keyboard connects directly to the computer.

You can simultaneously record MIDI from the MPK Mini on one track while recording audio from the microphone on another track — composing and recording at the same time in one take.

7

Final Power Sequence & First Session

The correct startup and shutdown order for every session

▼
1

Correct Power-On Sequence

Always power on in this order: (1) Computer → (2) Scarlett Solo (via USB connection) → (3) Yamaha MG10XU → (4) Yamaha HS5 monitors. Monitors should always come on last because any power-on surge or pop from the devices upstream will go through the signal chain — monitors on last means they never receive that transient. Set the Scarlett Solo monitor knob to minimum before powering monitors on, then slowly raise it to your working level.

Critical: Never power on the HS5 monitors before all upstream devices (interface, mixer) are fully on and stable. Power transients through speaker drivers can damage the tweeter — always monitors last on, monitors first off.
2

Correct Power-Off Sequence

Reverse the power-on order: (1) HS5 monitors → (2) MG10XU → (3) Scarlett Solo / computer. Before switching off monitors, reduce the monitor volume knob on the Scarlett Solo to minimum. This prevents any shutdown pop from reaching the speakers.

3

Set Your Listening Level Reference

Calibrate your monitoring level: play a familiar commercial track you know well (a professionally mixed song in a similar genre to what you're making). Set the Scarlett Solo monitor volume to a level where it feels comfortable but you could hold a conversation without shouting — approximately 75–80dB SPL. Note the physical position of the monitor knob. Always mix at this level. Consistency in monitoring level trains your ears and makes mixes translate more reliably.

4

Make Your First Recording

Create a new project in your DAW. Add an audio track (input: Scarlett Solo Input 1, mono). Arm it for recording. Speak or sing into the microphone while watching the input meter — aim for peaks around −18 to −12 dBFS (the meter should reach into the upper third but never hit 0dBFS / clip). Hit record, perform your take, stop recording. Listen back through the monitors or headphones. You've just made your first studio recording.

Pro tip: Record your first take, then immediately zoom into the waveform. If it looks like a solid block with flat tops — it's clipping (too loud). If it looks like a thin line — it's too quiet. Aim for a waveform that uses about 50–70% of the available height, with dynamic peaks that clearly exceed the average level. This is healthy gain staging.

Your studio is now fully operational. The complete signal path is: Voice/Instrument → Microphone → XLR cable → MG10XU mixer (gain, EQ, effects) → TRS cable → Focusrite Scarlett Solo (conversion) → USB → DAW (recording/editing) → USB → Scarlett Solo (playback) → TRS cables → HS5 monitors. Every piece of gear has a defined role, and every connection is balanced and noise-rejecting. Welcome to StudioGhar.

Production Workflow

1

Choose Your Mic

For natural, airy, detailed vocals — use the AT2020 condenser with 48V phantom power. For punchy, direct, room-rejecting takes (or recording late at night) — use the SM58. The SM58's proximity effect also adds warmth at close range (under 10cm).

2

Set Up the Recording Space

Position the mic in the acoustic treatment corner. Hang a thick duvet or moving blanket behind the vocalist if you don't have full foam coverage. Record vocals at night when ambient noise is lowest — air conditioning, traffic, and appliances all add noise floor.

3

Set Gain & Record Dry

Record the vocal track completely dry — no reverb or effects in the DAW recording chain. Gain stage so the loudest moments peak at −10 to −6 dBFS. Always record multiple takes (comping) and choose the best lines from each.

4

Edit & Comp the Vocal

In the DAW, cut together the best phrases from multiple takes. Zoom into breaths and trim excessive breath sounds. Use pitch correction (free: MAutoPitch, GarageBand Flex Pitch) to correct notes while keeping a natural feel. Keep the correction subtle — 50–60% strength.

5

Process the Vocal Chain

Standard vocal plugin chain: EQ (cut below 80Hz, reduce boxiness at 300–400Hz, add air at 10kHz) → Compression (4:1 ratio, medium attack ~10ms, fast release) → De-esser (tame harsh S sounds at 6–8kHz) → Reverb (short plate or room, 1–1.5s decay) → Delay (1/8th note rhythmic delay, low in the mix).

6

Double-Track & Harmonise

Record the same vocal line again on a second track — pan one 20% left, one 20% right. Even slight timing and pitch variation creates natural width. Sing 3rd and 5th harmony intervals on additional tracks and stack them subtly underneath the lead.

Beat Production Workflow

1

Load a Drum Instrument

In your DAW, create an instrument track and load a drum plugin. Free options: BeatSkillz RetroKomp, Sitala, or use GarageBand/Cakewalk's built-in drum kits. Map your MPK Mini MK4's 8 pads to kick, snare, open hi-hat, closed hi-hat, clap, snap, shaker, and rimshot.

2

Programme the Drum Pattern

Start with 4/4 at 140–150 BPM for trap, 85–95 BPM for boom-bap. For trap: kick on beat 1, snare on beat 3, hi-hats in triplet rolls. For boom-bap: kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4. Use the DAW's step sequencer or record live hits from the MPK pads.

3

Build the 808 Bass

Download OBXD or Vital (free) as a bass synthesiser. Create a bass instrument track. Use the MPK Mini keys to play bass notes that follow your kick pattern — the 808 bass note sustains under the kick for that characteristic sliding bass sound. Pitch-bend with the joystick for slides between notes.

4

Add Melody (Sample or Play)

Option A: Sample a vinyl record or old track (for personal/non-commercial use), chop it in your DAW, and pitch it to key. Option B: Load a piano or Rhodes plugin on the MPK Mini and play your own melodic loop. Typically 2–4 bars that loop throughout the beat.

5

Record the Rap Vocal

Use the SM58 for rap — its proximity effect thickens the voice and its cardioid rejection keeps the beat from bleeding in even at high headphone volumes. Record multiple doubles and ad-libs. Keep the lead centred, pan doubles ±30%, and bury ad-libs −6 to −10 dB below the lead.

6

Mix & Master

Side-chain the 808 bass to duck slightly under the kick. Apply heavy compression to the vocal (8:1 ratio, fast attack). Use a limiter on the master bus targeting −1 dBFS ceiling, −8 to −6 LUFS integrated loudness for streaming.

Pop Production Workflow

1

Start with Chord Progression

Use the MPK Mini's Chord Mode to instantly play full chords with one finger — try I–V–vi–IV (C–G–Am–F in C major) which underlies hundreds of pop hits. Load a piano or Rhodes plugin. Record the progression at the intended BPM using a metronome in the DAW.

2

Programme the Drum Track

Pop drums are typically sample-based — use Addictive Drums Free or the DAW's built-in drums. Standard pop pattern: kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, 8th-note closed hi-hats, open hi-hat on the 'and' of beat 4. Add a crash on bar 1 of each section. Pre-drop section: remove kick, leave snare and hi-hat only.

3

Layer Synth Pads & Leads

Using Vital or the DAW's built-in synths, add a pad sound playing the same chords for warmth and width. Add a lead synth or arpeggio for the hook. Pop arrangements layer 4–8 instrument tracks — piano, pad, bass, lead, counter-melody. The MPK Mini handles all of these via different instrument tracks.

4

Record Vocals with AT2020

Pop vocals need the AT2020 — the SM58 doesn't have the high-frequency extension and detail that modern pop requires. Enable Air mode on the Scarlett Solo. Record the lead, doubles, and harmonies. Pop vocal production stacks many layers: lead → doubles (×2) → thirds harmony → fifths harmony → octave above or below.

5

Mix for Width & Impact

Pop mixes are wide and bright. Pan elements: kick and bass centred, snare centred, hi-hats slightly off centre, synth pads hard left/right, guitar doubles L/R. Use a stereo widener on pad tracks. Keep the kick and bass mono. EQ for brightness — pop sounds brighter than you think when monitoring.

6

Arrange Verse, Chorus, Bridge

Pop arrangement: Intro (4 bars) → Verse (16 bars) → Pre-Chorus (8 bars) → Chorus (16 bars) → Verse (16 bars) → Chorus → Bridge (8 bars) → Final Chorus → Outro. Add and remove elements to create energy dynamics — drop elements before the chorus and bring them all back on beat 1.

EDM Production Workflow

1

Build the Kick-Bass Foundation

EDM starts from the bottom up. Load a 909 or 808 kick drum from a sample pack. Then create the bassline on a synth (Vital: use a sine wave oscillator, pitch it to root note). The kick and bass must share frequency space carefully — low-cut the bass above 60Hz where the kick lives, or sidechain the bass to duck on every kick hit.

2

Programme the Drum Pattern

House/Techno: 4-on-the-floor kick (all 4 beats), open hi-hat on offbeats, snare/clap on 2 and 4. Drum & Bass: kick on 1 and 3, breakbeat snare on beat 2 (ahead of the beat), ultra-fast hi-hat rolls. Use LFO Tool (free) or native sidechaining for pumping compression on synth pads.

3

Design Synth Sounds

Open Vital. Learn three fundamental synth types: Supersaw lead (multiple detuned sawtooth oscillators + filter sweep for the drop), Pluck/Arp (fast envelope decay, high filter cutoff), Pad (long attack, long release, chorus effect). Use the MPK Mini's 8 knobs to control filter cutoff and resonance in real time while recording.

4

Build the Arrangement — The Drop

EDM structure revolves around the drop. Intro (16 bars) → Build-up (8 bars, increasing energy, rising white noise sweep) → DROP (full arrangement hits) → Break (remove elements) → Build-up again → Drop → Outro. The contrast between the stripped-down break and the full drop is where all EDM energy comes from.

5

Use Effects as Instruments

Reverb, delay, filters, and automation are compositional tools in EDM — not just finishing touches. Filter-sweep a synth down to nothing in the bar before the drop. Automate reverb size growing throughout the build. Use a high-pass filter sweep (automate a filter rising from 20Hz to 20kHz over 8 bars) — a classic build effect.

6

Master for Loudness & Club Systems

EDM is mastered loudly: target −6 to −4 LUFS for club tracks. Use a multi-band compressor on the master to keep the low end tight (compress 20–200Hz harder than mids). Use a brickwall limiter (0 dBTP ceiling). Check your mix in mono — if it collapses, fix mono compatibility in the bass.

Acoustic Recording Workflow

1

Tune & Prepare the Guitar

Use a free chromatic tuner plugin (e.g. gStrings on Android or a DAW tuner) to tune precisely. Change strings 24 hours before recording — new strings are bright and take time to settle. Wipe down with a cloth after playing to extend their lifespan. Loose nut or bridge? Tap the guitar body while recording paused — any sympathetic resonance will appear on the recording.

2

Mic Placement for Acoustic Guitar

Use the AT2020 at the 12th fret, 20–30cm away, angled toward the soundhole at about 30°. This is the universally flattering position. For more body/warmth: move mic closer to the soundhole (15–20cm, angle away to reduce boominess). For more attack/brightness: move toward the fret area, aim at the 9th–10th fret.

3

Add Vocal on a Separate Pass

For best isolation, record guitar and vocals separately — record guitar first to a click track, then overdub vocals. If you want the live energy of recording both at once, record them simultaneously but mic the guitar at the 12th fret and the vocal from above (angled down) to maximise separation between the two mics.

4

Light Processing — Less is More

Acoustic recordings should sound natural. EQ: cut the low-mid mud at 200–400Hz (often called "cardboard box" sound), high-pass below 80Hz, and gently add air at 10–12kHz. Compression: very light, 2:1 ratio, slow attack (50ms) so the pick transients remain audible. Add a small room reverb (pre-delay 20ms, decay 1.2s) for realism.

5

Layering for Fullness

Record the same guitar part twice — pan one take slightly left, the other slightly right (±25%). Even with slight timing variations, this creates a rich, wide stereo image. Add a third "capo" take in a higher position for brightness. These layered acoustic guitars are the foundation of most indie-folk recordings.

6

Add Piano and Strings via MIDI

Use the MPK Mini to add piano, organ, or orchestral strings via VST. A simple piano pad playing the same chords as the guitar adds warmth without competing. Keep the MIDI arrangement sparse — acoustic music breathes through space and silence. Fewer notes, longer sustains.

Lo-Fi Production Workflow

1

Jazz Chord Progression

Lo-fi uses jazz-influenced chord extensions — 7th, 9th, and 11th chords. Try: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7 → Am7 (ii–V–I–vi in C major). With the MPK Mini Chord Mode off, play these voicings manually: for Dm7, play D-F-A-C. Play slowly, with slight timing imperfections — lo-fi is human, not quantised.

2

Choose the Right Piano/Keys Sound

Load Spitfire LABS "Soft Piano" or the free Keyzone Classic Rhodes plugin. Lo-fi piano sounds warm, slightly muffled, and intimate — like a Wurlitzer or Rhodes electric piano recorded to cassette. Avoid bright, modern concert grand piano sounds — they're too clinical for the genre.

3

Programme Jazzy Drum Samples

Lo-fi drums are sampled, compressed, and dusty. Download a free lo-fi drum pack (Cymatics, Looperman). Use the MPK Mini pads to trigger a hi-hat, kick, snare, and rim shot. Swing quantise the pattern to 55–65% swing in your DAW — this introduces the characteristic shuffle feel. BPM: 75–95.

4

Add Bass & Ambient Layers

Play a simple walking bass line on the MPK Mini keys (one note per beat, following the chord roots) using a double-bass or upright bass VST (free: DSK The Grand in bass mode). Add an ambient pad underneath — low volume, long reverb, barely audible. This adds subconscious warmth.

5

Apply Lo-Fi Processing

The lo-fi sound is created by deliberate degradation. Stack these effects: → Tape Saturation (free: Chow Tape Model) — adds harmonic warmth and subtle compression. → Vinyl Noise (free: RC-20 Retro Color Intro or iZotope Vinyl free) — adds crackle, hiss. → Low-pass filter (cut above 12–15kHz) — reduces brightness. → Bit reduction (slight) — adds digital warmth.

Podcast Production Workflow

1

Mic Selection for Voice

For voiceover and podcast: the SM58 is the professional's choice — its proximity effect adds warmth to the voice, its rejection keeps the room out, and its presence peak adds intelligibility. The AT2020 suits voices that are naturally deep and warm — it captures more room detail so the acoustic treatment must be thorough. Test both and keep whichever sounds more like "you."

2

Two-Person Setup via MG10XU

Plug the SM58 into Channel 1 for Host and the AT2020 into Channel 2 for Guest. Set each channel's gain independently. Both mics feed the main mix, which records to the DAW as a stereo mix (or two separate mono tracks if your DAW allows). Plug ATH-M50x into the Scarlett Solo for host monitoring, ATH-M20x into the MG10XU phones output for guest.

3

Record & Edit in the DAW

Record in WAV 44.1kHz, 24-bit — never compress to MP3 while recording. Edit the raw recording: cut long silences, remove "ums" and "ahs" strategically, delete background noise events. Use the DAW's waveform view to quickly locate and remove unwanted sounds without listening through everything.

4

Process the Voice Chain

Podcast voice chain: → Noise Gate (silence periods below −40dB) → High-pass filter at 80Hz → EQ: cut 200–400Hz (mud), boost 2–4kHz (intelligibility), slight cut 5–8kHz (sibilance) → Compression: 4:1, fast attack, auto release → Limiter: −1 dBTP ceiling. Free chain: all built into Audacity, GarageBand, or Reaper.

5

Add Intro Music & Sound Design

Use the MPK Mini to compose a short intro/outro jingle using a piano or synth VST — 15–30 seconds. Or use royalty-free music from Pixabay, Free Music Archive, or ccMixter. Fade the music under the voice rather than cutting it abruptly. Use the DAW's automation to draw volume curves.

6

Export & Publish

Export the finished episode as MP3 at 128kbps mono (for voice-only) or 192kbps stereo (if music is prominent). Target −16 LUFS integrated for podcast platforms (Spotify, Apple Podcasts normalise at −16 LUFS). Use a free loudness meter (Youlean Loudness Meter) to verify before export.

Bollywood Production Workflow

1

Build the Rhythm Foundation

Contemporary Bollywood typically combines a live tabla feel with programmed drums. Download a free tabla sample pack (freesound.org has extensive collections) and map hits to the MPK Mini pads. Pair with a programmed kick and snare for the "filmi" fusion sound. Common Bollywood time signatures: 4/4 (Dadra or Teen Taal at medium tempo), 6/8 for slower romantic songs.

2

Set Up the Tanpura Drone

Every Indian music recording needs a tonal reference. Use the free iTanpuri app (mobile) or Tanpura Droid to play a continuous drone in the song's key. Route this through a spare channel on the MG10XU into the headphone cue mix for the vocalist — they hear the drone while recording but it stays out of the main DAW recording.

3

Orchestral & Melodic Layers via MIDI

Load Spitfire LABS (free) for strings, brass, and choir. Load DSK Indian DreamZ (free) for sitar, sarangi, bansuri, and shehnai. Use the MPK Mini to compose the melodic prelude (mukhda), verse (antara), and orchestral interludes. Keep string voicings in three parts — first violins (melody), second violins (counter-melody), cello/viola (harmony).

4

Record the Lead Vocal

Use the AT2020 — enable Air mode on the Scarlett Solo for the upper harmonic extension that Indian classical vocals require. Record with the Tanpura drone in the cue mix. Indian vocals involve microtonal ornaments (meend, gamak, murki) — use minimal pitch correction if any, or set it to a chromatic scale rather than a western diatonic scale, which will clash with microtonal ornamentation.

5

Add Chorus Vocals & Backing

Layer 3–4 doubles of the main hook. Pan them in a tight cluster (L15%, R15%, centre). Add a sampled male or female chorus from Spitfire LABS "Voice of Britain" for a large choir feel. Bollywood arrangements typically have prominent background vocal stabs (choral hits on beats) which you can replicate with short-decay choir patches.

6

Electronic & EDM Elements (Modern Bollywood)

Contemporary Bollywood (post-2010) mixes Indian elements with EDM drops. After the classical mukhda, a drop can hit with an EDM-style synth bass and four-on-the-floor kick. Produce this using the same EDM workflow — the contrast between classical verse and electronic chorus is the defining feature of modern filmi production.
